A mail triage service

One creator gets more than 100 collaboration offers a week. This ranks the ones worth answering, refuses the fraud, and drafts the replies she asks for — without ever sending anything she hasn’t read.

She uses it every day. Once a day it checks her mailbox, scores whatever arrived, and updates an interface she opens in her browser: offers ranked by what they are actually worth, the money pulled out of each pitch, and the fraud held separately from the work. It has tracked more than 750 deals so far, and costs a few cents a message to run.

It sends only what she has confirmed

The part that checks her mail can only read it, a limit enforced by Google, so a mistake there has nothing to misuse. Marking a message read or archiving it uses a separate permission, used when she presses those buttons. Sending uses a third, and only for a reply she has read, edited as she likes, and confirmed.

None of them is the full-mailbox permission Google issues, which would grant sending and deleting as a side effect of wanting to read. Taking the narrower ones cost weeks. For a tool pointed at someone else’s livelihood, that is the right trade every time.

Her mail stays on her side

It runs on a personal machine. No server, no hosting provider, no account system, no archive of her correspondence sitting somewhere waiting to be breached. Message text is kept on that machine for at most 45 days, to draft replies, and access can be revoked by her, at any time, without asking anyone. The privacy policy says exactly what it touches.

Changes get measured against the whole inbox

When she declines an offer she writes why, and those reasons are the most valuable signal the system has. They are also biased: people only explain themselves when something annoys them, so the complaints overstate how common a problem really is.

So a proposed ranking change gets measured against the full corpus before it ships, not against the pile of complaints that prompted it. Several ideas that looked obviously worth building turned out to move a handful of items out of hundreds. Knowing that in advance is the difference between a week spent well and a week spent politely.

It writes; she decides

For anything worth a response, it drafts a reply in her voice and puts it in front of her. Nothing goes out unread, nothing is scheduled, nothing is autonomous. The tool’s job is to make the decision cheap, not to make it for her.

See it

The demo is the real interface running on invented offers — the ranking, the filters, the search. No one’s actual mail appears in it, and none ever will.

It is called Collab-Sorter. The code is private and stays that way — it runs against a real person’s live mailbox.
